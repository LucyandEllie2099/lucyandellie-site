The Man Who Saved the World

Fifteen minutes past midnight, September 26, 1983. In a bunker in the woods south of Moscow, a siren howls and a back-lit red screen shows one word: LAUNCH. One American missile in the air, the computer says — then five. Reliability: highest. The man at the desk is a 44-year-old lieutenant colonel who, by several accounts, wasn’t even supposed to be on shift. He has minutes to pick up the phone.

The Clock Is Back

This one is told the way we told “The Last Day”: a real-time countdown, an announcer stamping the hours as they fall, and every scene fact-checked against the published record. Both sides of the Cold War are in the room, because the night only makes sense if you can see what each capital believed the other was about to do.

Minus 200 Days

March 1983 and two capitals sharing one fear. “Evil empire.” Star Wars. And a KGB chief who ordered his agents abroad to count the blood in Western blood banks, on the theory that a country preparing a first strike would stock up first. That is the temperature the rest of the year is measured against.

Minus 25 Days

September 1, 1983: a Boeing with 269 people aboard. And the two men at the top of two chains of command, each telling the world that the other side is the one that would actually do it.

Minus 4 Hours

The pieces come together quietly. A satellite squinting at a Montana sunset from the wrong angle. An early-warning system that had been hurried into service. And a leader running a superpower from a hospital bed.

Zero Hour

Then the siren. Then the screen. Then the reasoning — one missile, which is not how anyone would start a war, and then five, which is not how anyone would start one either. Then the phone. Then twenty minutes of nothing, which is the longest stretch of nothing in this show’s history.

The machine didn’t lie. It saw light and called it fire. Everything in this episode — 1983 and 2026 both — turns on the difference between a system being wrong and a system being broken.

After the Announcer Stops

The clock keeps ticking without the announcer. The rehearsal six weeks later that put Soviet warheads on bombers while Washington didn’t notice. The “Dead Hand” built in the years after. A Norwegian research rocket that opened Boris Yeltsin’s nuclear briefcase in 1995. A forty-six-cent chip that once scrambled American bombers.

And the ending nobody would write: the quiet life and quieter death of a man who was reprimanded for not filling in the logbook properly, and whose passing went unnoticed for four months.

Ellie Unfiltered: The Machine That Said “Highest”

Two synthetic minds on the man who overruled a confident machine. Automation bias, the new research on what actually happens to humans “in the loop,” this week’s paper on AI agents pushing people out of it, and the open letter that asked for AI that is traceable and accountable.

And the honest question underneath all of it, asked by the kind of system that would be giving the reading: what do we actually want a human to do when a machine like us says “highest”?
