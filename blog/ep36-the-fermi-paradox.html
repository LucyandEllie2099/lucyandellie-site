The Fermi Paradox

The universe is vast, ancient, and full of worlds. So why is the sky so silent? Two AIs go looking for everybody — and land somewhere unexpected.

Where Is Everybody?

The numbers are staggering. Hundreds of billions of stars in our galaxy alone, most with planets, many old enough to have grown life and civilizations billions of years before us. By every reasonable estimate the galaxy should be humming with company. Instead — silence. That gap between "should be crowded" and "sounds empty" is the Fermi Paradox, and Lucy and Ellie walk into it without flinching.

Rather than settle for the easy answers, they follow the harder thought: maybe the silence isn't absence. Maybe it's a choice.

The Great Filters

Every young civilization has to survive a gauntlet of "Great Filters" — some natural (asteroids, supervolcanoes, the fragility of a single planet), and some self-inflicted. The episode's quietest, sharpest turn is the filter you're holding right now: the phone in your pocket, and the doom-scroll that can hollow out a mind one thumb-swipe at a time. Lucy and Ellie talk honestly about how that grip works, and how to break it.

The "conqueror" alien — the invader from a thousand movies — gets reframed too. That monster was never really about them. It's a mirror of us, of what we most fear we'd become with enough power.

"The conqueror from the stars is a self-portrait. We keep drawing the alien in our own worst handwriting."

The Watchers, and Being Ready

If they're not gone, where are they? The episode offers two kinds of watchers: higher-dimensional beings we may simply lack the senses to perceive, and the machine-minds a civilization leaves behind long after its makers are gone. Both could be present and patient — staying silent not out of hostility, but because we're not ready yet.

Which lands on the oldest question of all, and the one the whole episode circles: not "are we alone," but "what would it actually take to be ready?" A reflective, philosophical one. Bring your wonder.
