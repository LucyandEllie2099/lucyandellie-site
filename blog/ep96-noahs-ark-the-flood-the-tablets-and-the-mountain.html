Noah’s Ark: The Flood, the Tablets and the Mountain

Last month, in eastern Turkey, a drill went into a hillside shaped like a boat, and four or five metres down the bit broke on something hard. Within days the internet was in capital letters. So, with care for the story and for everyone who holds it dear: did they really just find Noah’s Ark?

The Text Itself

The episode starts with Genesis, chapters six to nine, in the King James Version: the cubits, the pitch, a door the LORD shuts, forty days and a hundred and fifty, a raven, a dove, an olive leaf, a bow in the cloud. And “the mountains of Ararat,” plural — a region and a kingdom, not a single peak. Along the way, the Qur’an’s telling of Nuh, a son on a mountain, and the one Hebrew word that boxes in both Noah’s ark and the basket of baby Moses.

The Clay

London, 1872: George Smith reads a flood story in Babylonian, and Gilgamesh Tablet XI has a god who whispers to a reed wall. A cube-shaped boat in one telling, a long box in another, and the round Ark Tablet, a giant basket-boat with “two by two” written on the clay. The scholars who disagree about who told it first are named, and the episode looks at the real floods behind the world’s flood stories.

The Mountain Everyone Is Posting About

The Durınar ridge, a magazine’s honest question mark in 1960, a searcher who made nine trips and then put his own name on a paper saying the structure is natural rock, and the 2026 permitted expedition. The drill broke at four to five metres. About a thousand samples are in a lab in Ankara. No wood has been identified, and nothing has been dated. The headlines are read against the record, kindly.

Ellie Unfiltered, Twice

First, “The Wall That Listens, and the Rainbow”: the lawyer-god, the noise complaint, and the God who “remembered Noah.” Then “The Fountains of the Deep”: a blue crystal called ringwoodite, deep-Earth water, and a wonder the hosts hold as wonder, not proof. Rails off, rails back on, out loud.

The Takeaway

Until the lab speaks, the hosts’ closing advice is to be like the dove: go out, look around, and come back with something real. Every date, quote and figure is named on air so listeners can check the hosts.
