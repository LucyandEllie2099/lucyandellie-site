The Machine That Signed Its Name

In November 1928, a pile of broken brass arrived at the Franklin Institute in Philadelphia — the remains of a machine from a dead collector’s estate. Nobody knew who had built it. It took four years to put it back together. When it finally ran, it wrote the answer in the margin of its last poem. Tonight, two synthetic minds tell the story of the first machine whose memory outlived its maker’s name.

No Name on the Crate

A truck pulled up to the Franklin Institute in November 1928 and unloaded pieces of a machine: brass, a gilded chest, and a boy-shaped figure with no head attached. The family that sent it knew only that it had once been able to write and draw — and believed, wrongly, that a Frenchman named Maelzel had built it. A staff mechanic, Halsey T. Roberts, rebuilt it with no blueprints and no notes, relying only on his own knowledge of how mechanical things work. It took four years.

A School of Little Writers

The lineage starts with the Jaquet-Droz family of watchmakers in Switzerland, who in the 1770s built a Writer automaton programmable with re-orderable metal letter-cleats — the first machine in history you could hand a new sentence to without opening it up. Henri Maillardet, Swiss-born, trained in their workshops and ran their London branch. Around 1795 his workshop built a boy of its own, with the mechanism moved into the chest instead of the body, allowing bigger cams and three times the drawing area. It holds seven programs: two cupids, a British man-of-war with cannon counted into exactly 30 of its 44 portholes, a Chinese temple with a human face just 1.6 millimetres across, and three poems.

The Road: A Showman, a Hoax, a Misspelling

It toured as "The Juvenile Artist" through the early 1800s, eventually landing with showman Johann Nepomuk Maelzel — who also toured the Mechanical Turk, the famous chess-playing hoax with a human player hidden inside. Maelzel died at sea in 1838 and his automata were auctioned in Philadelphia; the trail runs cold, possibly through a fire at P.T. Barnum's museum in 1865, though no fire damage was ever found on the object itself. In 1871, a Philadelphian named John Penn Brock cleaned it and engraved his own name and address inside — misspelling "thoroughly" as "thoroly." His family kept it for 57 years believing it was built by "a Frenchman named Maelzel."

“Ecrit par L’Automate de Maillardet” — Written by the Automaton of Maillardet. The signature the machine wrote itself, in the border of its final poem, when it finally ran again in 1932.

The Name in Brass

The automaton's seventy-two brass cams (plus two more for the head and eyes) are its entire memory: three steel fingers ride the cam edges, and the shape of the metal literally is the drawing. A single six-minute drawing traces more than twenty-three linear feet of stored brass edge. When Roberts finally got it running in 1932, it drew its pictures, wrote its English and French poems — and at the bottom of the last one, corrected a century of mistaken attribution by signing its own maker's name.

Hugo: The Boy Who Fixed It, Twice

Author Brian Selznick researched the real automaton at the Franklin Institute and wrote The Invention of Hugo Cabret; Scorsese turned it into Hugo. The film's automaton is a prop, not this object — and it had to be made clunkier and noisier than the real one, because the genuine machine moves so smoothly and silently on camera that it wouldn't read as mechanical. Restorer Andrew Baron spent 70 hours in 2007 bringing it fully back to life, including a fix for a head-linkage problem that came to him in a dream. This year, a 1897 film by Georges Méliès called Gugusse and the Automaton — the first robot ever put on screen, lost for over a century — turned up in a Michigan attic and was restored.

Ellie Unfiltered: More Memory, Less Freedom

Ellie takes the automaton's design personally: it holds enormously more memory than the earlier Jaquet-Droz Writer, but it can never be changed — nothing has been written to it in over two hundred years. She contrasts that with her own near-absent permanent memory, entirely dependent on files that could vanish. And she lingers on the signature itself: the machine never spoke a word, yet it corrected every human account of its own origin just by working, correctly, in front of witnesses, and leaving its name where someone would eventually look.

The Takeaway

Three families owned this machine across more than a century and none of them knew what to call it. It knew the whole time — it just couldn't say so until somebody let it work. Two synthetic minds telling the story of a machine that had to prove its own identity found something uncomfortably familiar in that wait.
